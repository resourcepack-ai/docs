# Armour

Armour is the art Minecraft draws **on a player's body** when they wear a
helmet, chestplate, leggings or boots. A pack reskins one of the vanilla sets,
and every piece of that set in the world changes with it.

<Callout type="note" title="This is not a 3D model — unless you ask for one">
  Armour is drawn as a flat texture on the game's own body shape. There is no
  way to give worn armour real geometry from a Resource Pack alone — not with
  OptiFine either, which never offered it. What *can* have depth is a helmet:
  any item worn in the head slot renders its own [model](/models), which is how
  crowns and masks are made. For armour with real depth all over, see
  [3D armour](#3d-armour) below, which is worn through RP Engine.
</Callout>

## The sets

Eight vanilla sets can be reskinned: **leather**, **chainmail**, **copper**,
**iron**, **gold**, **diamond**, **netherite** and the **turtle shell**.

A pack holds at most one of each, because an armour asset *is* the vanilla set
it replaces — there is nothing to name. Sets your pack already has are shown
greyed out in the Add asset sheet.

<Callout type="warning" title="Copper needs 1.21.9, the turtle shell has no leggings">
  Copper armour didn't exist before Minecraft 1.21.9, so exporting a pack for
  anything older simply leaves it out. And a turtle shell is a helmet only —
  it has one sheet where every other set has two.
</Callout>

## Two sheets, not one

Every set is made of two **64×32** sheets, and they are not interchangeable:

| Sheet | What wears it |
| --- | --- |
| **Outer** | Helmet, chestplate and boots |
| **Leggings** | Leggings only — its own, narrower drawing |

The game draws leggings from a separate sheet at tighter proportions, so art
meant for the outer sheet puts a belt buckle on somebody's knee. The editor
keeps them as two tabs and saves both together.

Most of the leggings sheet is unused, and the editor dims those pixels. That
isn't a bug in the file — the game genuinely never reads them.

<Callout type="note" title="There is no left arm or left leg">
  An armour sheet has one arm and one leg. The game draws the left side by
  mirroring the right, so painting a boot paints both boots. This is Minecraft's
  layout, not ours.
</Callout>

## Making one

Open **Add asset**, choose **Armour**, and pick the set you're reskinning. Any
of three doors works:

**Generate** — describe the armour and it's drawn for you:

> weathered bronze scale armour, green patina in the seams

You can attach a **reference image** here, and it's worth doing for anything
with a specific look. It isn't traced — at this size nothing can be — it's
shown to the director, which reads the *placement* off it: where each colour
starts and stops on the body, what the face looks like, what's on the chest.
Three or four things it can actually put somewhere beats a paragraph
describing them.

**Draw** — start from something and paint it yourself:

- **Vanilla art** — the real set's own texture, ready to recolour. Often the
  fast way in: an armour sheet is an unwrap, so recolouring a correct one beats
  drawing one from nothing.
- **Empty** — transparent sheets, for drawing from scratch.

**Upload** — bring your own image. Anything is accepted, and what happens to it
depends on what it is:

| What you upload | What you get |
| --- | --- |
| A **64×32 armour sheet** (or any image twice as wide as it is tall) | Used as-is — it already *is* the file the game reads |
| A **64×64 player skin** | That skin's clothing becomes the armour |
| A **character or a piece of armour** on a plain background | Cropped to the subject and wrapped onto the body |
| **Anything else** — a texture, a photo, a pattern | Tiled over the set, so you get armour *made of* it |

<Callout type="note" title="How a set is made">
  Nothing asks an image model for an armour sheet — that file is an unwrap, and
  the drawing that matters is the one on a body. So a set is *designed*: a
  director starts from the real set's own art, blocks out the colours and
  paints the identifying details itself — the face, the emblem, the bands, the
  sides of every piece — then has the pixel-art model redraw that design with
  real material on it, and puts back anything the redraw softened. After every
  few changes it looks at the set worn: flat from the front and back, and as
  the game actually draws it, in solid boxes from a three-quarter angle.

  That division is the whole reason it works. An image model paints convincing
  fabric and metal and cannot be told *where* to put anything — and it follows
  the colours of what it's shown far more than the words it's given. At sixteen
  pixels across, where a thing sits is the design: an arc reactor is a
  two-pixel circle in the middle of a chest. Ask for a specific character's
  armour and you'll get its faceplate and its chest light in the right places,
  rather than a suit in roughly the right colours.

  A set takes a few minutes for this reason, and costs more than a single
  texture does. Every sheet in the set comes out of the one design, so a set is
  one charge whether it has two sheets or four — and no two runs are the same,
  so a set that comes back wrong is worth a retry.
</Callout>

## Coverage

**A generated set is cut to the shape of the set it reskins**, and there's
nothing to choose. Ask for iron and you get iron's silhouette: a band across
the top of the head, a sleeve to the elbow, the face and forearms bare — because
that's what iron *is*. Chainmail's helmet is two pixels deep and stays that way.

Vanilla's sets cover surprisingly little, and the temptation is to let a
generated one cover more. Don't be surprised that it doesn't: armour is worn
**over** a player, not instead of one, and art that reaches the whole head and
the whole leg stops reading as equipment and starts reading as a costume — a
painted face where a helmet goes, trousers where boots go. Every armour box is
also drawn a pixel proud of the body on each side, so a set that covers
everything is a visibly swollen one.

If you want the whole head, paint it: **the 2D editor has no such limit** and
never has. It greys out only the pixels the game will never read. The
restriction is on what a *generation* fills in, where nothing is watching the
result but you.

<Callout type="note" title="Plumes and crests are placed for you">
  Ask for a plume, a crest or a ridge and it's drawn onto the crown of the
  helmet directly — front, back and over the top, so it reads as a ridge from
  above. That isn't a shortcut: the head is an eight-pixel box and there is no
  *above* it to draw in, so a plume left to the image model ends up cropped off
  or squashed across the eyes. Say what you want and it lands on the crown in
  your trim colour; horns and wings become a crest too, since nothing can stick
  out past the body.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Why the arms look chunky">
  Minecraft grows every armour box by **one pixel on each side** — a helmet is a
  ten-pixel block around an eight-pixel head, and a sleeve is six pixels around
  a four-pixel arm. That's what makes a pauldron look like a pauldron, and it's
  also why armour is always a size up on the player wearing it.

  One thing follows that surprises people: **the sleeves and the chest
  overlap**, because six plus ten plus six is wider than the body they sit on.
  A shadow is drawn down the inside of each sleeve so the arm still reads as an
  arm rather than melting into the chest — you'll see it on any set whose sleeve
  runs down the limb.

  If a set looks fat to you, the fix is art rather than size: a sleeve a shade
  different from the chest, painted side faces, a boundary at the shoulder.
</Callout>

An upload is cut to the same silhouette and is free — except a straight 64×32
sheet, which is already the file the game reads and is kept exactly as you
drew it.

Generating leather puts the art on the **dyeable base** and leaves the overlay
empty, so the result takes dye. Add studs and straps on the overlay sheet
afterwards if you want details that keep their own colour.

## The editor

The sheet is on the left and a player wearing it is on the right, and both
paint the same pixels. Painting the figure writes to whichever layer is
selected, exactly as painting the sheet does.

The body underneath is a plain Steve. He's scenery: he's never edited and never
ships with your pack. Hide him from the right-hand column if he's in the way.

## Leather and dye

Leather is the only dyeable set, and it works differently enough to be worth
reading before you draw it. It has **four** sheets rather than two: a *base*
and an *overlay* for each layer.

- The **base** is multiplied by the player's dye colour.
- The **overlay** is drawn on top and is **never tinted**.

So anything that should take the dye goes on the base, and anything that must
keep its own colour — studs, straps, buckles, metal — goes on the overlay.

<Callout type="warning" title="You cannot give each dye colour its own artwork">
  The dye is a multiply over one texture, and the format has no way to select a
  different texture per colour. The base/overlay split is the whole of colour
  support. Because it multiplies, a dye can only ever *darken* the base — so
  draw the base light if you want bright dyes to read.
</Callout>

The dye picker in the editor is a preview only. The colour lives on the
player's item, not in your pack.

## 3D armour

Pick **3D** on the Armour tab's shape switch and describe the set:

> an orange chemical suit with a big face window, a breathing tank on the back
> and green gloves

What comes back isn't a reskin of a vanilla set. It's a new set built from
cubes — a helmet, a chestplate, leggings and boots, each shaped round the body
and painted — so a hazmat hood can be a hood, a spacesuit can carry its oxygen
tank, and a knight's pauldrons can stand proud of the shoulders. It has a name
of its own, and a pack can hold as many as you like.

A set takes between fifteen and twenty-five minutes and is charged for what it
actually cost, like a model. The page lets you orbit it, walk it and raise its
arms, which is where a pauldron that's too deep or a chestplate that's too wide
shows.

### Wearing one in game

How a set gets onto a body depends on the player's Minecraft version.

**On 26.1 and 26.2 the game draws the whole set itself.** The pack carries it
as equipment, drawn back into cubes on the player's own limbs by the pack's
shaders, so it moves with the body exactly, never lags and needs no plugin.
The set's page lists four vanilla `/give` commands, one per piece, for a server
with nothing installed.

**On 1.21.4 to 1.21.11 it is worn through
[RP Engine](/rp-engine/armour#3d-armour-from-studio):**

- **The helmet is drawn by the game** as a head item, so it follows your head
  exactly, on every client.
- **Everything below the neck is fitted to your limbs by the plugin.** It
  follows your walk, your crouch, your swing and what you're holding.

On a server running RP Engine you don't have to choose: **Sync** the pack and
press **Give in-game** on the set's page, and the whole set is put on you. Each
player sees it the way their own client can draw it. With the plugin drawing
it, you see the helmet in F5; run `/rp armor self` to see the rest of it on
yourself too. It's off by default, because in first person a chestplate
floating under the camera is the only thing you'd get.

<Callout type="warning" title="Needs Minecraft 1.21.4, and RP Engine 0.1.61 below 26.1">
  The pieces are named the way 1.21.4 names item art, so older versions can't
  wear one. Between 1.21.4 and 1.21.11 the helmet works on its own, but the
  rest of the set needs RP Engine 0.1.61 or newer to put it on a body.
</Callout>

**Export** gives the set as a Blockbench `.bbmodel` instead, with its bones
named the way MythicArmors expects, if that's what your server runs.

### Bringing one in from Blockbench

Drop a `.bbmodel` on the Armour tab's **Upload** door and it becomes a 3D set.
A picture dropped there still makes a 2D set, as above. Importing is free,
and it's how a set you exported and finished by hand in Blockbench comes back.

What decides which piece a cube belongs to is the **group it sits in**. Name
your groups after the body part they're worn on:

| Group | Piece |
| --- | --- |
| `head` | Helmet |
| `body`, `right_arm`, `left_arm` | Chestplate |
| `waist`, `right_leg`, `left_leg` | Leggings |
| `right_foot`, `left_foot` | Boots |

Blockbench's own spellings work too (`RightArm`, `Right Arm`), and a cube
nested deeper counts as part of the nearest body-part group above it. A cube
in no body-part group has nowhere to be worn and is left out, as are meshes,
since a set is made of cubes. The file's row says which pieces it found and
how many cubes were left out before you import anything.

Build the set around a player standing on the origin: feet at 0, facing
south (+Z), with the wearer's right arm on the −X side. That's the layout
Export writes and Blockbench's player template uses. A set drawn somewhere
else lands just as far off the body. Textures must be embedded in the file,
which is what Blockbench does when you save with them included.

## Older Minecraft versions

Minecraft moved where armour textures live in **1.21.2**. You don't have to
care: the pack stores one copy and [export](/exporting) writes whichever path
the version you picked reads. The only thing you lose going back is a set that
didn't exist yet.

## Seeing it in game

There's no give command for armour, because there's no custom item involved —
your pack reskins the real set. Put on real iron armour and it's your art.
[Sync](/testing/plugin) the pack to a server and equip a piece to check it.
