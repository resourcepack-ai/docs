# Armour

Armour is the art Minecraft draws **on a player's body** when they wear a
helmet, chestplate, leggings or boots. A pack reskins one of the vanilla sets,
and every piece of that set in the world changes with it.

<Callout type="note" title="This is not a 3D model">
  Armour is drawn as a flat texture on the game's own body shape. There is no
  way to give worn armour real geometry from a Resource Pack — not with
  OptiFine either, which never offered it. What *can* have depth is a helmet:
  any item worn in the head slot renders its own [model](/models), which is how
  crowns and masks are made.
</Callout>

## The sets

Eight vanilla sets can be reskinned: **leather**, **chainmail**, **copper**,
**iron**, **gold**, **diamond**, **netherite** and the **turtle shell**.

A pack holds at most one of each, because an armour asset *is* the vanilla set
it replaces — there is nothing to name. Sets your pack already has are shown
greyed out in the Add asset sheet.

<Callout type="warning" title="Copper needs 1.21.9, the turtle shell has no leggings">
  Copper armour didn't exist before Minecraft 1.21.9, so exporting a pack for
  anything older simply leaves it out. And a turtle shell is a helmet only —
  it has one sheet where every other set has two.
</Callout>

## Two sheets, not one

Every set is made of two **64×32** sheets, and they are not interchangeable:

| Sheet | What wears it |
| --- | --- |
| **Outer** | Helmet, chestplate and boots |
| **Leggings** | Leggings only — its own, narrower drawing |

The game draws leggings from a separate sheet at tighter proportions, so art
meant for the outer sheet puts a belt buckle on somebody's knee. The editor
keeps them as two tabs and saves both together.

Most of the leggings sheet is unused, and the editor dims those pixels. That
isn't a bug in the file — the game genuinely never reads them.

<Callout type="note" title="There is no left arm or left leg">
  An armour sheet has one arm and one leg. The game draws the left side by
  mirroring the right, so painting a boot paints both boots. This is Minecraft's
  layout, not ours.
</Callout>

## Making one

Open **Add asset**, choose **Armour**, and pick the set you're reskinning. Any
of three doors works:

**Generate** — describe the armour and it's drawn for you:

> weathered bronze scale armour, green patina in the seams

You can attach a **reference image** here, and it's worth doing for anything
with a specific look. It isn't traced — at this size nothing can be — it's
shown to the director, which reads the *placement* off it: where each colour
starts and stops on the body, what the face looks like, what's on the chest.
Three or four things it can actually put somewhere beats a paragraph
describing them.

**Draw** — start from something and paint it yourself:

- **Vanilla art** — the real set's own texture, ready to recolour. Often the
  fast way in: an armour sheet is an unwrap, so recolouring a correct one beats
  drawing one from nothing.
- **Empty** — transparent sheets, for drawing from scratch.

**Upload** — bring your own image. Anything is accepted, and what happens to it
depends on what it is:

| What you upload | What you get |
| --- | --- |
| A **64×32 armour sheet** (or any image twice as wide as it is tall) | Used as-is — it already *is* the file the game reads |
| A **64×64 player skin** | That skin's clothing becomes the armour |
| A **character or a piece of armour** on a plain background | Cropped to the subject and wrapped onto the body |
| **Anything else** — a texture, a photo, a pattern | Tiled over the set, so you get armour *made of* it |

<Callout type="note" title="How a set is made">
  Nothing asks an image model for an armour sheet — that file is an unwrap, and
  the drawing that matters is the one on a body. So a set is *designed*: a
  director starts from the real set's own art, has the pixel-art model paint
  the material and colour over it, and then places the identifying details
  itself — the visor, the emblem, the bands — looking at a render of the set
  worn after every few changes and fixing what it sees.

  That division is the whole reason it works. An image model paints convincing
  metal and cannot be told *where* to put anything, and at sixteen pixels
  across, where a thing sits is the design: an arc reactor is a two-pixel
  circle in the middle of a chest. Ask for a specific character's armour and
  you'll get its faceplate and its chest light in the right places, rather than
  a suit in roughly the right colours.

  A set takes a few minutes for this reason, and costs more than a single
  texture does. Every sheet in the set comes out of the one design, so a set is
  one charge whether it has two sheets or four — and no two runs are the same,
  so a set that comes back wrong is worth a retry.
</Callout>

## Coverage

Both Generate and Upload ask how much of the body your art may cover, and the
answer changes the shape of the result more than anything else on the sheet.

| | What you get |
| --- | --- |
| **Whole body** (default) | A closed helm, sleeves to the fingertips and complete leggings — every pixel the game will draw |
| **Vanilla shape** | Cut to exactly what the real set covers, so the face and forearms show through like the real thing |

Vanilla's own sets cover surprisingly little: iron's helmet is a band across the
top of the head, chainmail's is two pixels deep, and both leave the face bare.
That's iron's design decision, not yours — so a set is drawn to the whole body
unless you ask for the vanilla silhouette back. The 2D editor has always let you
paint the whole head by hand; this is the same freedom on the generated side.

<Callout type="note" title="Plumes and crests are placed for you">
  Ask for a plume, a crest or a ridge and it's drawn onto the crown of the
  helmet directly — front, back and over the top, so it reads as a ridge from
  above. That isn't a shortcut: the head is an eight-pixel box and there is no
  *above* it to draw in, so a plume left to the image model ends up cropped off
  or squashed across the eyes. Say what you want and it lands on the crown in
  your trim colour; horns and wings become a crest too, since nothing can stick
  out past the body.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Where Whole body stops, and why the arms look chunky">
  Minecraft grows every armour box by **one pixel on each side** — a helmet is a
  ten-pixel block around an eight-pixel head, and a sleeve is six pixels around
  a four-pixel arm. That's what makes a pauldron look like a pauldron, and it's
  also why armour is always a size up on the player wearing it. Two things
  follow:

  - **Boots stop at the shin.** Boots live on the outer sheet, *outside* the
    leggings, so a boot reaching the hip would permanently hide the leggings you
    drew in the same set. This is the one place Whole body doesn't reach.
  - **The sleeves and the chest overlap**, because six plus ten plus six is
    wider than the body they sit on. A shadow is drawn down the inside of each
    sleeve so the arm still reads as an arm rather than melting into the chest
    — you'll see it on any set that covers the whole limb.

  Hands are covered. They used to be left bare to keep the arms slim, which
  meant a full-body suit ended in bare fists poking out of its own gauntlets;
  the shadow above is what solves that properly.
</Callout>

An upload is free, and takes the same coverage choice — except a straight 64×32
sheet, which is kept exactly as you drew it and isn't cut at all.

Generating leather puts the art on the **dyeable base** and leaves the overlay
empty, so the result takes dye. Add studs and straps on the overlay sheet
afterwards if you want details that keep their own colour.

## The editor

The sheet is on the left and a player wearing it is on the right, and both
paint the same pixels. Painting the figure writes to whichever layer is
selected, exactly as painting the sheet does.

The body underneath is a plain Steve. He's scenery: he's never edited and never
ships with your pack. Hide him from the right-hand column if he's in the way.

## Leather and dye

Leather is the only dyeable set, and it works differently enough to be worth
reading before you draw it. It has **four** sheets rather than two: a *base*
and an *overlay* for each layer.

- The **base** is multiplied by the player's dye colour.
- The **overlay** is drawn on top and is **never tinted**.

So anything that should take the dye goes on the base, and anything that must
keep its own colour — studs, straps, buckles, metal — goes on the overlay.

<Callout type="warning" title="You cannot give each dye colour its own artwork">
  The dye is a multiply over one texture, and the format has no way to select a
  different texture per colour. The base/overlay split is the whole of colour
  support. Because it multiplies, a dye can only ever *darken* the base — so
  draw the base light if you want bright dyes to read.
</Callout>

The dye picker in the editor is a preview only. The colour lives on the
player's item, not in your pack.

## Older Minecraft versions

Minecraft moved where armour textures live in **1.21.2**. You don't have to
care: the pack stores one copy and [export](/exporting) writes whichever path
the version you picked reads. The only thing you lose going back is a set that
didn't exist yet.

## Seeing it in game

There's no give command for armour, because there's no custom item involved —
your pack reskins the real set. Put on real iron armour and it's your art.
[Sync](/testing/plugin) the pack to a server and equip a piece to check it.
