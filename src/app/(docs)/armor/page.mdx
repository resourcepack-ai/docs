# Armour

Armour is what Minecraft draws on a player's body when they wear a helmet,
chestplate, leggings or boots. There are two kinds in Studio, picked with the
**2D / 3D** switch under **Shape**:

- **2D** reskins one of the vanilla sets. Every piece of that set in the world
  changes, and no plugin is needed.
- **3D** builds a new set out of cubes, so a hood can be a hood and a
  spacesuit can carry its oxygen tank. See [3D armour](#3d-armour).

## The sets

Eight vanilla sets can be reskinned: **Leather**, **Chainmail**, **Copper**,
**Iron**, **Gold**, **Diamond**, **Netherite** and the **Turtle Shell**. A pack
holds one of each, because a 2D armour asset *is* the vanilla set it replaces.
Sets your pack already has are greyed out in the Add asset sheet.

<Callout type="warning" title="Copper needs 1.21.9, and the turtle shell is a helmet">
  Copper armour arrived in Minecraft 1.21.9, so a pack exported for an older
  version leaves it out. The turtle shell has no other pieces.
</Callout>

## How the file is laid out

Each set is drawn on **64×32** sheets: one shared by the helmet, chestplate and
boots, and a separate one for the leggings. The editor hides this from you
with one tab per piece (**Helmet**, **Chestplate**, **Leggings**, **Boots**);
on a piece's tab the other pieces are dimmed so you can't paint them by
accident.

The sheet has only one arm and one leg. The game mirrors them for the other
side, so painting one boot paints both. Pixels the game never reads are dimmed
in the editor.

## Making one

Open **Add asset**, choose **Armor**, keep **Shape** on **2D** and pick the set.

**Generate**: describe it, for example "weathered bronze scale armour, green
patina in the seams". You can attach a **reference image**; the AI uses it to
decide where colours and details go on the body (the faceplate, the emblem on
the chest). A set takes a few minutes and costs more than a single texture,
but it's one charge however many sheets it has. No two runs are alike, so a
set that comes back wrong is worth another try.

**Blank**: start from **Vanilla art** (the real set, ready to recolour, often
the fastest way in) or **Empty**.

**Upload**: bring an image. What you get depends on what it is:

| You upload | You get |
| --- | --- |
| A **64×32 armour sheet** (or any image twice as wide as it is tall) | Used exactly as it is |
| A **64×64 player skin** | That skin's clothing becomes the armour |
| A **character or a piece of armour** on a plain background | Cropped and wrapped onto the body |
| **Anything else**, such as a texture or a photo | Tiled over the set, so the armour is made of it |

Uploads are free.

### What a generated set covers

A generated set follows the shape of the set it reskins. Iron covers what iron
covers: a band round the head, sleeves to the elbow, the face bare. That keeps
it looking like armour worn over a player rather than a costume. If you want
more coverage, paint it in the editor, which has no such limit.

Plumes, crests, horns and wings are drawn onto the crown of the helmet, since
nothing can stick out past the body's boxes.

<Callout type="note" title="Why armour looks a size up">
  Minecraft draws each armour box one pixel bigger than the body on every side,
  so the sleeves and the chest overlap a little. A sleeve a shade different
  from the chest, or a clear line at the shoulder, keeps the arm reading as an
  arm.
</Callout>

## The editor

The sheet is on one side and a player wearing it is on the other, and you can
paint either. The plain Steve underneath is only there for preview; he never
ships with your pack.

## Leather and dye

Leather is the only dyeable set, and it has two sheets per layer, switched with
the **Base** / **Overlay** pill beside the piece tabs:

- The **base** is multiplied by the player's dye colour.
- The **overlay** is drawn on top and never tinted.

Put anything that should take the dye on the base, and anything that must keep
its own colour (studs, straps, buckles) on the overlay. A generated leather set
paints the base and leaves the overlay empty.

<Callout type="warning" title="Dye can only darken">
  The dye multiplies the base, so it can only make it darker, and there's no
  way to give each dye colour its own artwork. Draw the base light if you want
  bright dyes to show.
</Callout>

The **Dye** picker in the editor is a preview. The colour lives on the
player's item, not in your pack.

## Seeing 2D armour in game

[Sync](/testing/plugin) the pack, then choose **Wear in-game** on the set's card
in the pack (or press **Sync** in the editor): you're dressed in the real vanilla pieces, now showing
your art. Without a plugin, put on the vanilla armour and you'll see it.

Minecraft moved armour textures in 1.21.2. You don't have to think about it:
[export](/exporting) writes whichever path your chosen version reads.

## 3D armour

Pick **3D** under **Shape** and describe the set:

> an orange chemical suit with a big face window, a breathing tank on the back
> and green gloves

You get a new set built from cubes: a helmet, chestplate, leggings and boots,
each shaped round the body and painted. It has its own name, and a pack can
hold as many as you like. You can attach pictures to build one like them.

A set takes 15 to 25 minutes and is charged for what it actually cost, like a
model. Its page lets you orbit it, walk it and raise its arms, which is where a
chestplate that's too wide shows up.

### Wearing one in game

You need **Minecraft 1.21.4 or newer**. How the set reaches the body depends on
the player's version:

| Version | How it's drawn | Needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **26.1 and 26.2** | By the game itself, through shaders in your pack. Moves exactly with the body | Nothing: the set's page lists a vanilla `/give` command per piece |
| **1.21.4 to 1.21.11** | The helmet by the game; the rest fitted to the limbs by [RP Engine](/rp-engine/armour#3d-armour-from-studio) | RP Engine 0.1.61 or newer |

On a server running RP Engine you don't have to choose: **Sync** the pack and
press **Give in-game**, and each player sees the set the way their client can
draw it. When RP Engine is drawing the body pieces you won't see them on
yourself in first person; `/rp armor self` shows them in third person (F5).

**Export** downloads the set as a Blockbench `.bbmodel`, with bones named the
way MythicArmors expects.

### Importing from Blockbench

Drop a `.bbmodel` on the Armor tab's **Upload** door and it becomes a 3D set
(a picture still makes a 2D set). Importing is free.

Each cube is worn on the piece its group is named after:

| Group | Piece |
| --- | --- |
| `head` | Helmet |
| `body`, `right_arm`, `left_arm` | Chestplate |
| `waist`, `right_leg`, `left_leg` | Leggings |
| `right_foot`, `left_foot` | Boots |

Blockbench's own spellings work too (`RightArm`, `Right Arm`), and a cube
nested deeper belongs to the nearest body-part group above it. Cubes in no
body-part group are left out, and so are meshes. The upload list shows what was
found before you import.

Build the set around a player standing on the origin: feet at 0, facing south
(+Z), right arm on the −X side. That's Blockbench's player template and what
Export writes. Save with textures embedded in the file.

## On Bedrock

2D armour carries over to the `.mcpack` under Bedrock's names for each set, and
looks the same on both editions.

3D armour works for Bedrock players on a server running
[RP Engine](/rp-engine) and Geyser, following their arms and legs as they move.
Pieces handed out before the server had RP Engine 0.1.66 show as paper; hand
them out again.
