# Generating with AI

Generation exists to get you past the blank grid. It is not a vending
machine: the useful mental model is a fast, tireless collaborator who sketches
in seconds and has no ego about you painting over it.

## What actually happens

<Steps>
  <Step title="Textures">
    Always generated as a **16×16 grid of flat colours** — the resolution
    vanilla Minecraft uses — regardless of the resolution you picked for the
    asset. If you asked for 32 or 64, that 16×16 draft is block-upscaled with
    nearest-neighbour, the same way real HD packs handle a low-res original.
    You get the extra pixels; the AI didn't fill them in. See
    [Texture editor](/editors/textures).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Models">
    Generated as cubes with **semantic texture keys** rather than a bespoke
    atlas — `#case`, `#screen`, `#fur` — which are then mapped to vanilla
    block textures like `minecraft:block/iron_block`. That's why a generated
    model already looks like Minecraft instead of like a render: it's built
    out of materials the game already ships.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Icons">
    Generated as a bitmap glyph at the size you pick, ready to assign to a
    character. See [Custom icons](/editors/icons).
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Writing a prompt

Be specific about **material, colour and structure**. Be vague about pixels —
the model can't count them for you, and asking it to will cost you the parts
it's good at.

<CardGroup>
  <Card title="Works" icon="sparkles">
    *weathered copper roof tiles, blue-green patina, strong diagonal grain*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Works less well" icon="wrench">
    *a nice block, 4px border, dark at the bottom, make it pop*
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

Some things worth naming explicitly:

- **The material.** "Oxidised copper" gets you further than "green metal".
- **The wear.** New, weathered, cracked, mossy — this is most of what makes a
  texture look like it belongs in Minecraft.
- **The structure.** Planks, tiles, bricks, panels, woven. Give the eye a
  repeating unit.
- **What it sits next to.** "reads next to deepslate" is a real constraint and
  the model will use it.

<Callout type="tip">
  Prompt for the thing, not for the fix. If a texture is close but too busy,
  say "same block, fewer distinct colours, calmer surface" rather than trying
  to describe the finished pixels.
</Callout>

## Iterating

You can send an existing asset back for another pass with a new instruction —
that path sees the current pixel grid, so it edits rather than starts over.
This is usually better than re-rolling the original prompt: you keep the
palette and silhouette you already liked.

<Callout type="warning">
  Iterating still costs a generation, and it can drift. If you've painted an
  asset by hand, duplicate it first — the AI edits the whole grid, not just
  the part you mentioned.
</Callout>

## Knowing when to stop generating

The honest rule: once the silhouette and palette are right, switch to the
editor. Hand-fixing eight pixels takes fifteen seconds and gets you exactly
what you meant; three more prompts take two minutes and get you something
slightly different each time.
