# Dialogs

A dialog is the screen a server opens on a player — added in Minecraft
**1.21.6**. It is not a container: it has no slots, and its buttons are real
buttons that run real commands.

What makes it worth building is that a dialog is made of **text**. Its title,
the lines in its body and every button's label are text components, and a text
component can be a font glyph. So a pack can put a whole screen of cards,
controls and icons in the body, drawn as glyphs, with every button on it still
clickable — and what comes back is a screen that looks like Minecraft made it.

<Callout type="warning" title="A dialog needs a plugin, and 1.21.6">
  The picture is in your resource pack. The dialog itself is not — it is
  datapack data, which a resource pack cannot carry. [RP
  Engine](/rp-engine) is what writes it on the server and opens it. On
  anything older than 1.21.6 the game has no dialog screen at all; a
  [GUI](/guis) is the older way to do the same job and works everywhere.
</Callout>

## Making one

Dialogs are made under **Add asset**, through either door:

- **Generate** — say what the screen is for and it is designed for you. See
  [Generating one](#generating-one).
- **Draw** — a dialog starts with its title, a line of words and two buttons
  that are **placed anywhere**, so you can drag them wherever you want them,
  plus the game's own **Close** button in its footer. Build the rest in the
  editor. See [Editing one](#editing-one).

Either way it looks like a screen Minecraft itself made. Nothing is painted
behind it: the words, buttons and icons sit straight on the darkened, blurred
world, in the game's own font with its shadow. The title is the one the game
draws in its header, with the game's warning button beside it, and the button
at the bottom is the game's own, in its footer. When there is more on the
screen than fits, the game scrolls it and shows its own scrollbar on the right
— so a guide can be as long as it needs to be.

Dialogs made before 30 September 2026 may be in the older **UI style**, a
drawn 256×256 menu. Those still open, edit and sync as they always did — see
[Older UI-style dialogs](#older-ui-style-dialogs) — but a new dialog is not
made that way any more.

## What it is made of

A dialog can be up to **388 pixels wide** (nearly the whole width of a small
screen) and about **1,000 pixels tall**, and it is built out of rows, columns
and grids of these pieces:

| Piece | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Button** | The game's grey 20-pixel button. **Selected** makes it gold, the way a store marks the category you are in; **Greyed out** is the game's disabled button; the label can have its own colour, and a sidebar's buttons can read from the left. A button colour tints the same button, bevels and all. |
| **Link** | A line of clickable words, for a guide's contents. The current page is white and underlined, and sub-pages can be indented. |
| **Text** | Words in the game's own colours. Titles are twice the size, headings bold. Colour codes change colour mid-line, the way chat plugins write them: `Ping: &a42` (`&a` green, `&c` red, `&6` gold, `&e` yellow, `&b` aqua, `&7` grey, `&l` bold, `&r` back to normal, `&#55ff55` any colour). |
| **Panel button** | The light button of a menu built like an inventory screen: a white rim and a shaded bottom edge, 20 pixels tall. Made for sitting on a dark panel. |
| **Chip** | A dark, see-through box for a balance, a count or a rank tag. |
| **Setting row** | A full-width button with a value on the right, green when on and red when off. |
| **Tile** | A dark card with a word, an icon and a caption, and a state: ready (gold), claimed (dimmed, ticked) or locked (padlocked). |
| **Check line** | A ✔ or a ✘ in front of a word, for what a rank includes. |
| **Label and value** | A label on the left and a value on the right, for receipts and stats. |
| **Page of text** | Markdown-lite: `# Title` twice the size, `## Heading` in bold, `### Heading` in gold, `- bullets`, `> notes`, `**words**` in gold, `---` a rule. |
| **Icon** | Any vanilla item, a player's head, one of your pack's own item sprites or textures, or one of the editor's drawn icons — at 16, 24, 32, 48 or 64 pixels, bare or **in a slot** (the dark well an item sits in). A head is that player's skin as it was when the dialog was saved. |
| **Progress bar**, **QR code**, **Divider**, **Space** | As they sound. A QR code carries its own link and opens it when clicked. |

Any row, column or grid can also sit on one of the GUI's own surfaces:

| Surface | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Panel** | A light, raised card — a white rim and a thick bottom edge. The card in a list of kits, warps or punishment reasons. |
| **Dark panel** | A dark section, for a group of controls: a heading and a stack of panel buttons. |
| **Slot** | The dark well an item sits in, around anything. |

Their frames are added to the box's padding for you, so a 32-pixel item in a
slot on a panel padded 3 comes out as the 52-pixel card you would expect. (The
older **Shade**, **Deep shade** and **Gold** are see-through, over the world.)

Anything with a tooltip is clickable, and so is every button, link, tile and
setting row. A whole row or column can be one button too — tick **Clicking** in
its panel, which is what makes a card clickable anywhere on it. Tooltips are
written as described in [Tooltips](#tooltips).

### Settings screens: cards, controls and themes

The pieces above make the big servers' stores and guides. A dialog can also be
built the way a polished **settings screen** is: a sidebar of buttons under a
search box, and beside it **cards** — each with a heading in heavy capitals, a
small square button in its corner and a line under the heading — holding
**rows** of a small icon, a name, one line of description and a **control** on
the right. The same parts make a profile, a leaderboard, a quest log, a
moderation menu or a shop that looks designed rather than typed.

**Every dialog has a theme.** Cards, sidebar buttons, switches, sliders and
highlights are drawn in it, and words on a card take its colours by themselves
— a name in the card's label colour, a description in its quieter one. Pick one
of fourteen under **Theme** in the dialog's settings — Stone, Slate,
Deepslate, Nether, Ocean, Jungle, End, Royal, Oak, Parchment, Ice, Cherry,
Redstone and Sand — or choose any **Card** and **Accent** colour and the rims,
shadows and wells are worked out from those two. A dialog made before themes
existed looks exactly as it did.

| Surface | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Card** | The theme's card, its corners cut at 45°. The **Card** in Layout adds one with a heading, a reset button and a divider already on it. |
| **Well** | A darker box sunk into a card: a product in a shop, a balance, a stat. |
| **Band** | A strip of highlight behind one row — your place on a leaderboard, a search match, the quest that is ready. |

| Control | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Switch** | On or off, with the word beside it if you want one. |
| **Segmented** | Two to four choices side by side, the chosen one lit. Each choice is its own click. |
| **Slider** | A value with arrow buttons either side and a track with tick marks. Each arrow is its own click. |
| **Dropdown** | The chosen one of a list, with a caret. |
| **Checkbox**, **Stepper** | A box with a tick; minus, a number and plus. |
| **Search box**, **Text box** | A sunk field with a magnifier (or any icon) and grey words, or words that were typed. |
| **Sidebar button** | A light button with a small icon, lit in the accent when it is the page you are on, with an optional badge ("3", "NEW"). |
| **Icon button** | A small square button: reset, refresh, info. |
| **Theme button** | The theme's 16-pixel button with an icon — a **Clear**, a **Buy**, a **Claim**. Selected lights it in the accent, and a **Button colour** paints it in any colour, its edges and words worked out to match. |
| **List option** | One row of a list to pick from, the chosen one lit. |
| **Badge** | A small solid tag: NEW, -20%, a count. |
| **Display text** | The heavy capitals a card's heading is set in, for big numbers too. |

**Small icons.** Nearly every label on a settings screen has a nine-pixel icon
in front of it, the height of a line of words. The icon picker's **Small** shelf
has more than a hundred of them — interface marks, settings categories, game
things and flags — and any text can carry one before its words. Items and heads
are still there for the bigger pictures: a product, a reward, a player.

### Player settings

A dialog is a picture, drawn when it is saved — so a switch on it is a picture
of a switch, on for everybody. A switch, segmented control, slider, stepper,
checkbox or dropdown can instead show **each player's own setting**: type a name
under **Player setting** in its panel (lower-case, like `show_sidebar`).

Every state of the control is then drawn, and the game shows each player the
one their setting names. Clicking it changes their setting — a switch flips, a
segment picks its own value, an arrow moves the slider one step — and the
dialog opens again, straight away, showing the new state. Nothing else on the
server is needed for that; the clicks are wired for you, and settings are kept
per player, across restarts.

Your other plugins read a setting as `%rpengine_var_show_sidebar%` through
PlaceholderAPI. Inside a dialog, `{show_sidebar}` is its value like any other
placeholder. A player can only set a setting some dialog on the server shows,
and only to one of the values it offers.

<Callout type="warning" title="Needs RP Engine 0.1.62 or newer">
  An older RP Engine opens the dialog with every state of a bound control
  drawn in the same place, the default on top, and a click on it only gets an unknown-command reply.
  Update the plugin on the server.
</Callout>

## Pages

A settings screen with a sidebar is really several screens that look alike: the
same sidebar with a different button lit, and different cards beside it. A
dialog can be exactly that — up to **ten pages**, with a click turning between
them. A store's categories, a guide's chapters and a tab bar work the same way.

**The quickest way is from the sidebar.** Click any of its buttons, and its
panel shows **A page for every button**: each button with the page it will open
— *this page* for the one that is lit, *new page* for the rest. **Create pages**
makes them. Every button gets a page of its own, named after it, and goes there
when clicked. The box beside the buttons becomes **the part that changes from
page to page** — outlined in green on the screen — and everything outside it,
the sidebar included, stays the same on every page. Click a button in that list
later to open its page.

The **Pages** strip above the screen has a tab for each page. Click one to edit
it, and double-click to rename it. The first tab, with the house, is the page
the dialog opens on. **+** adds a page — a copy of the one you are on, ready to
change — and the buttons at the right of the strip move, duplicate or delete the
page you are on.

Once a dialog has pages:

- **Edit anything outside the outlined box** — rename a sidebar button, change
  a heading above it, move a button placed anywhere — and every page changes
  with it.
- **Edit inside it** and only this page changes.
- **The lit button looks after itself.** A button, sidebar button, link, list
  option or segment that goes to a page is lit on that page and on no other, so
  a sidebar shared by six pages lights the right one on each. Its **Selected**
  switch is replaced by a line saying which page lights it.
- **The theme is the dialog's**, so changing it changes every page.

Anything clickable can go to a page: set **When clicked** to **Go to page** and
pick one — or pick **New page** to make one for it there and then, a copy of
this page named after the button. A footer button can turn the page too, to give
every page a way back to the first.

To share a different part, select that box and tick **Changes from page to
page** in its panel. Everything outside it then becomes what this page has, on
every page, so the editor asks first. Untick it and every page is its own whole
screen; they no longer follow each other's edits.

**In game, each page is a dialog of its own.** The first is the dialog's own id
as always, `studio:settings`, and the rest add the page's: `studio:settings.audio`.
`/rp dialogs` lists them all, and `/rp dialog` opens any of them. A click that
goes to a page runs `/rp page studio:settings.audio`, which RP Engine answers by
opening that page straight away — no restart — with the same `{placeholders}`
the first page was opened with, so a punish menu about Steve is still about
Steve on its second page. Every player may run `/rp page`, because the click is
theirs, but it only opens a page the dialog they are looking at links to.

**A dialog with pages stays open when it is clicked.** The page you are on
stays on screen until the next one replaces it, so turning a page never flashes
the world or throws your cursor back to the middle. Its footer button still
closes it (through RP Engine, `/rp page close`), and so does Escape; a click
that runs a command leaves it open, so a store can sell several things in a
row.

<Callout type="warning" title="Needs RP Engine 0.1.66 or newer">
  An older RP Engine opens every page with `/rp dialog`, but a click that
  should turn the page only gets an unknown-command reply. Update the plugin on
  the server.
</Callout>

## Editing one

A dialog opens in a builder laid out like the GUI editor: tools down the left,
the **Components** panel beside them, the screen in the middle and the selected
piece's settings on the right. The screen is the game's, at the size a real
monitor gives it (426×240 is a 1440p or 4K monitor on the Auto GUI scale,
480×270 a 1080p one), and it behaves like the game: the mouse wheel scrolls it,
hovering a clickable thing shows its tooltip, and **Clickable areas** (`A`)
shades where a click will land.

**Words, in place.** Double-click any words on the screen — a button's label, a
heading, a line of text, a sidebar button, the title in the game's header or a
footer button — and type straight onto it. **Enter** puts them there
(**Shift+Enter** starts a new line in a text or a page), **Escape** leaves them
as they were, and clicking anywhere else keeps what you typed. With a piece
selected, **Enter** or **F2** does the same, and so does **Edit words** in its
right-click menu.

**Adding.** The **+** (or `N`) opens **Add component**. Pick a shelf across
the top, or type in its search box to look across every shelf at once
(**Enter** takes the first match). Hover a component to see a picture of
exactly what arrives, drawn in the dialog's own theme.

- **Anywhere** (the first shelf) is pieces that are not in a row or a column at
  all — a **Button** exactly like the game's own (150 by 20, its words and its
  icon), an icon button, text, an item or a head in a slot, and a small note
  panel. They go wherever you click and sit on top of everything else. The
  button under **+** on the rail (`B`) picks up that **Button** in one click.
- **Blocks** are ready-made groups — a **settings card**, a setting row for
  each control, a **sidebar** under a search box, a **results header**, a
  **product grid**, **stat cards**, a **leaderboard** and a **quest** row, and
  the older ones: a **card** (an item in a slot, a name and a line in gold, on
  a panel), a **card list** and a **card grid**, a **profile card** with a
  player's head, a **control panel** of buttons, a **sidebar menu**, a **tab
  bar**, a **stats panel**, a **yes or no**, a row of reward tiles and a
  header bar. They are drawn in the dialog's own theme.
- **Controls** are the settings screen's switches, choices, sliders, fields and
  small buttons, each in the dialog's theme.
- **Basics** are the single pieces in the first table above.
- **Layout** is rows, columns and grids, the theme's **Card**, **Plain card**,
  **Well** and **Highlight band**, the three GUI surfaces, two columns side by
  side and a sidebar beside its content.

Pick one and it follows the pointer. A piece from **Anywhere** is dropped where
you click. Anything else shows a yellow bar where it will land — between two
pieces, inside a row or column, at the end — and a click puts it there. Hold
**Shift** to keep placing the same thing, press **Enter** to put it after
whatever is selected, or **Escape** to put it down. **Add inside** (on a box) or
**Add after** (on anything else) in the settings panel adds straight to the
selection instead.

**Selecting and changing.** Click anything in the preview or its row in
**Components**. The panel on the right edits it, in the order you usually want:
its words first, then — for anything a player clicks — what clicking it does
(**When clicked**, the same choices a footer button has) and its tooltip, then
how it looks: style, colours, icon, surface and size. A selected piece has blue
**grips**: drag the right edge to set its width, the bottom edge to set a box's
height, the corner for both (an icon's corner steps through its sizes).
Double-click a grip to go back to fitting the content.

The bar under the screen shows where the selection sits — **Screen › Card › Row
› Button** — and clicking any step selects it, which is the quickest way from a
word to the card it is on. The pointer turns into a cross of arrows over
anything you can pick up and drag.

**Moving.** Drag a piece in the preview and the yellow bar shows where it will
land, in any row or column. To carry a whole row or column, select it first
(the bar under the screen is easiest), then drag from anywhere inside it. The
dialog scrolls if you hold the piece near the top or bottom. In
**Components**, drop onto the top or bottom edge of a row to put the piece
before or after it, or onto the middle of a box to put it inside. **Alt** with
the arrow keys moves the selection one place; the plain arrow keys walk
through the pieces.

**Placing anywhere.** A free piece — from **Anywhere**, or a drawn dialog's two
starting buttons — is dragged to any spot, pixel for pixel. It snaps to the
middle and edges of the dialog, to other free pieces and to the edges of the
words and cards in the layout (hold **Alt** not to); the arrow keys nudge it a
pixel at a time (**Shift** for ten), and its panel has its exact **X** and
**Y**, measured from the dialog's top-left corner. The game draws a dialog only
inside its content — as wide as its **Content width**, centred on the screen —
so take a piece past a side and the dialog **widens to make room**, up to 388
pixels, while everything else stays where it is on screen; a dashed outline
shows the dialog's edge while you drag. Nothing can go above the top: that is
the game's title bar. Any piece already in the layout can join them: hold **Shift** while you
drag it and it comes out of its row or column, drawn where it will land, and
stays wherever you let go — or use **Place anywhere** in its panel or its
right-click menu to lift it out exactly where it is. **Back into the layout**
returns it to the end of the screen. A free button is clickable and has its
tooltip like any other, and where it lies over a card, a click lands on the
button. Components marks free pieces with a small cross of arrows.

**Components** shows every piece with its kind; boxes open and close, and a
double-click renames one (only you see the name). Right-click a piece there or
in the preview for **Edit words**, **Copy**, **Cut**, **Paste**, **Duplicate**,
**Group in a column**, **Put on a panel**, **Ungroup** and **Delete** — or use
`Ctrl+C`, `Ctrl+X`, `Ctrl+V`, `Ctrl+D`, `Ctrl+G` and `Ctrl+Shift+G`. Copied
pieces paste into your other dialogs too.

Click the empty space around the screen, or press Escape, to get back to the
dialog's own settings: its title, its width, its theme, its footer (one button,
or a yes/no pair) and how it behaves. Click the footer button itself to set its
words and what it runs. **JSON** shows the whole layout as the tree the
generator writes, and **Export** downloads the screen as a picture. Changes save
on their own; `Ctrl+Z` undoes and `Ctrl+Shift+Z` (or `Ctrl+Y`) redoes, and they
cover everything.

## Tooltips

Every clickable thing can explain itself on hover. Tooltips are written as
plain lines and coloured the way server menus colour theirs:

```
Tuesday - Free

Reward
∙ 250 Coins

Click to claim!
```

The first line is the title. A line above a list of `∙` items is that list's
heading, a line starting ✔ is green, one starting ⌀ or ✘ is red, and one
starting "Click" is yellow. The panel shows a preview, and hovering the piece
on the screen shows exactly what a player will see.

## What a button can do

One thing each, chosen under **When clicked** in a piece's panel, or in a
footer button's:

| Action | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **Run command** | The player runs it, with their own permissions. |
| **Suggest command** | A small box opens with the command typed in; they finish it and press **Run**. |
| **Open link** | The client asks before following it. |
| **Copy text** | Onto their clipboard. |
| **Go to page** | Turn to another of this dialog's [pages](#pages), or make a new one for it. |
| **Open another dialog** | Pick one of the pack's dialogs from the list — it opens straight away. |
| **Nothing** | Which, with "After a click: Close", is what an Ok button is. |

A command runs **as the player**, so it obeys their permissions. A button that
needs more than they have wants a command the server has set up for it, not a
higher-privileged button — there is no such thing.

**Suggest command** can't fill in the chat box, because Minecraft doesn't allow
it from a dialog a server opens: the game quietly drops the command. So the
suggestion opens in a small dialog of its own instead, with the command in a
text box and a **Run** button. A command that has to be signed, like `/msg`,
is caught by the game, which then offers to put it in the chat box.

Minecraft reads a whole dialog at once, and one action it cannot accept stops
the entire dialog opening. So a button whose action would be refused — a link
that does not start with `http://` or `https://`, an id that is not a valid
dialog id, an empty command — is sent with no action at all (it just closes
the dialog), and the editor marks it with a warning until it is fixed.

A button that opens another dialog from the same pack carries that dialog with
it, so it opens without a server restart. "Another id…" in the same list takes
any other dialog id, which the server has to know already — one of
Minecraft's, or one from a datapack.

## Inputs

A dialog can ask for something: a text box, a checkbox, a list to pick from or
a slider. Each has a **key**, and a button's command can write `$(key)` and get
what the player put there.

```
tp @s $(destination)
```

## Clickable in whole lines

The game finds a click by the 9-pixel line of text it lands on, so a clickable
thing is clickable on every line it covers at least half of. Two buttons
stacked with a few pixels between them split the line they share by which
covers more of it. A link in a contents list is clickable across its whole row
and halfway into the gaps above and below it, so the list has no dead spots.
**Clickable areas** in the editor shows the real shape.

Clicking anywhere else on the picture does nothing. The game would draw a white
frame round the whole dialog there, because the picture is one block of the
dialog's text and clicked text gets a frame; the pack hides it.

## Generating one

Say what the screen is for — "a settings menu with video, audio and chat", "a
pet menu where you can rename and summon your pet", "a crate menu for a prison
server" — and the generator designs it out of the pieces above: a sidebar when
there are categories to move between, cards with headings, setting rows with
the right control for each setting, grids of product wells, stat cards,
highlighted rows, small icons on every label and item pictures where a thing is
an item.

It chooses the **colours** from the subject, the way a designer would — a
theme whose mood fits it, or a card and accent colour of its own (a classroom
might come back chalkboard green with chalk-yellow highlights) — so different
screens come back in different colours. Name colours in the request and it
uses yours. If your pack has a **style guide** with a palette, the theme is
built from its colours instead.

Before the dialog is saved, the generator takes **a second look** at what it
drew: labels cut off, a card too narrow for what is in it, a screen too tall for
a 1080p monitor, a missing icon. It fixes what it finds, and keeps the fix only
if it is really better. That takes a little longer and costs no more.

A dialog is as wide as its content and has nothing behind it, so there is
nothing to choose but what you type, and it never uses the image model. Want a
progress bar — a streak, a tier, a goal? Say so in the request. A QR code is
added afterwards from the editor's **+**, where its link is yours to set. Every
number on a generated dialog — a balance, a price, a player count — is a
believable sample for you to change.

<Callout title="It names the buttons; you wire them">
  Nothing invents a command. A generated dialog arrives with its buttons named
  and described and their actions empty, because a command is a thing with
  consequences on your server — unless a control is bound to a
  [player setting](#player-settings), whose clicks are wired for you.
</Callout>

## Getting it onto a server

Pair the pack with a server running RP Engine and push it, the same way you
[test anything else](/testing/plugin). Then:

```
/rp dialogs                     # what the server has
/rp dialog studio:quest-board   # open one
```

The panel in the editor has the exact command for the dialog you are looking
at.

### A dialog about somebody

A punish menu is about the player being punished, so its title is "Punish
Steve" and every reason runs `mute Steve 1h`. Write that as a placeholder —
`{target}`, or any name — in the title, a tooltip, a footer button or the
command a click runs, and say what it is when you open the dialog:

```
/rp dialog studio:punish Admin target=Steve   # open it for Admin, about Steve
/rp dialog studio:punish target=Steve         # open it for yourself
```

A name the command does not give is filled the way RP Engine's HUD
placeholders are: `{player}`, `{ping}`, `{world}` and the other built-ins
describe the player the dialog is shown to, and PlaceholderAPI answers the
rest when it is installed. A name nothing knows is left as written. Plugins
pass the values through RP Engine's API instead.

Words drawn inside the dialog's picture — a name on a profile card, say — are
part of the picture, so they stay as you typed them.

<Callout title="A pushed dialog opens straight away">
  No restart for ordinary opening. On supported Mojang-mapped servers, RP Engine
  decodes the JSON with the server's own codec and sends the dialog directly,
  including multiline text. Other servers use the inline command path, with a
  registered datapack entry as a last fallback for content that path cannot carry.
  Push the updated resource pack before opening changed artwork.

  There is one thing a restart still adds. RP Engine also writes your dialogs
  into a datapack of its own, and after the next restart each one is a real
  `namespace:id` — which is what lets you name it from your own datapack, from a
  command block, or from a `minecraft:show_dialog` click event you wrote by
  hand. That half is registry data, and registry data is read when the server
  loads its world. `/minecraft:reload` does not do it: a reload rebuilds
  recipes, advancements, loot and tags, and this registry is built with the
  world instead.
</Callout>

## Older UI-style dialogs

Until 30 September 2026 a dialog could also be made in the **UI style**: a
256×256 picture with our own buttons, cards and a panel if you wanted one,
fitted to a single screen and edited in the same editor a GUI is. New dialogs
are not made that way any more. The ones that exist keep working exactly as
they did — they open, save, sync and open in game as before — and this is how
they are edited.

### Button guides

Instead of slot guides a UI-style dialog has **button guides**, and those are
the whole layout. Each dashed box is where the game will draw one of the
dialog's buttons. Paint inside a box and that artwork becomes the button's
face; leave it empty and the button keeps vanilla's grey widget and your words.
Everything outside the boxes is the panel. The boxes move when the dialog does
— add a button, change one's width, switch from two columns to three — so set
the buttons up first and paint into them second.

**Buttons go anywhere.** Choose **Button** in **+** and drag a box where it
should be, or just click for the game's own 150×20. It is drawn in the game's
own button and font, and what it does is set in its panel, under **When
clicked**, with its tooltip. Tick **Force to real button** and the same button
becomes the game's own widget instead, placed by the game with the dialog's
other real buttons (the dashed guides); untick it and it is drawn again where
it was.

To resize the dialog, click bare sheet so nothing is selected, then drag the
grips on its outline, or type a size under **Size** in the Dialog panel. The
background's frame and the strip behind the buttons keep their exact pixels
while the rest stretches. If a dialog has background hanging below its last
row of buttons, the panel offers **Pull it back in**.

Minecraft draws its own grey button first and your artwork on top, so any
transparent pixel inside a button's box shows that grey through; the canvas
shows the grey button underneath for exactly that reason. Buttons below the
panel keep the game's own outer pixel, which turns **white when the pointer is
over it**, so they light up on hover without a shader.

### Clickable areas

Any part of a UI-style dialog's picture — a reward tile, a shop card, a row in
a settings list — can be a **clickable area** with its own action and its own
[tooltip](#tooltips). Pick the **Areas** tool (A) from the rail and drag a box
over the picture; with the box selected, the panel sets what a click does and
its tooltip. Drag a box to move it, pull its grips to resize it, and press
Delete to remove it. Areas on a piece move with it when you drag the piece or
resize the dialog.

An area is clickable on every 9-pixel line its box crosses, across its exact
width, and the Areas tool shades the real clickable shape. The topmost line of
a UI-style dialog holds the picture itself and cannot be clicked. One
difference from a button: `$(key)` from an input is filled in for buttons only.

<Callout title="Saved before 26 September 2026?">
  A UI-style dialog saved before then only answers clicks and tooltips on its
  first clickable line in Minecraft 1.21.9 and newer. Open it in the editor,
  save, and Sync again to fix that.
</Callout>

### Fitting on the screen

Minecraft gives a dialog the screen's height less a 38-pixel header and footer,
and a UI-style dialog taller than that **scrolls**: its buttons start below the
edge of the screen. On the Auto GUI scale a 1440p or 4K monitor is only 240 GUI
pixels tall and a 1080p one 270. The **In game** box under Size says which of
those screens the dialog fits; only rows with artwork on them count, and
**Shrink to fit every screen** finds the tallest window that fits a 240-pixel
screen.

**Buttons** go either **in the panel** — a grid at the bottom of your art, in
as many columns as you choose, always right on the panel's bottom edge — or
**below the panel**, where Minecraft draws one or two under the dialog so your
art keeps a frame all the way round. **Sync saves pending edits first** and
stops if saving fails, so the game receives the layout you are looking at.

### If the picture sits a few pixels off

Where the game puts a UI-style dialog's body relative to its buttons is layout
inside the client, and it is not something a pack can read — so the placement
is worked out rather than looked up, and a Minecraft release that moves a
padding moves every drawn dialog with it. The **Nudge** fields in the Dialog
panel are the fix. Type the offset, save, look again. It is stored on that
dialog, so it survives everything else you do to it.
